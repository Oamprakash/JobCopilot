I've been building Android apps for over twelve years and now lead a team of engineers, which means I read a lot of resumes. Most of them get far less time than the person who wrote them imagines. Not because hiring managers are careless, but because there are many applications and a day job to get back to.

So here's what actually happens in that first half-minute, and what helps.

## 1. The top third decides whether I read the rest

I look at your current title, your current company, how long you've been there, and the first two bullets under it. If those match the role, I keep going. If they don't, the rest of the page rarely gets a chance.

**What helps:** put the work most relevant to *this* job in your first two bullets. The order of your bullets is not a historical record; it's an argument. Lead with your strongest point.

## 2. I'm looking for what you did, not what you were responsible for

"Responsible for the payments module" tells me where you sat. "Rebuilt the payments flow in Compose and cut checkout crashes after release" tells me what you changed. The second one is the one I remember.

**What helps:** start bullets with a verb that describes an action you took - built, led, moved, cut, shipped, replaced - and say what was different afterwards. If you have a real number, use it. If you don't, don't invent one; a clear before-and-after is enough.

## 3. A skills list of 40 items reads as noise

When I see every technology the person has ever touched, I learn nothing about what they're good at. Worse, I start wondering which ones they'd struggle with in an interview.

**What helps:** keep the list short and honest - the 10 or 12 things you'd be comfortable being questioned on. Make sure the ones the job asks for are there in the job's own words ("Jetpack Compose", not just "Compose UI").

## 4. Anything that looks inflated makes me doubt everything else

An unrealistic number, a title that doesn't match the company size, a skill that appears nowhere in the work history - any one of these makes me read the rest of the resume with suspicion. And if it gets past me, the interview usually finds it.

**What helps:** reword and reorder freely; never add. Tailoring means showing your real experience in the job's language. It doesn't mean claiming experience you'd have to bluff about later.

## 5. I check that you read the job

Generic resumes are easy to spot: the same summary sent to fifty companies, nothing that connects to what this team does. A two-line summary that names the kind of work the role involves, and a first bullet that matches it, does more than any design.

## The short version

- Lead with the work that matches this job.
- Say what you changed, not what you were assigned.
- Keep skills short, honest and in the job's words.
- Never claim what you can't defend in the interview.
